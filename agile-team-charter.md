# Agile Team Charter - Industry Placement

## 1. Purpose and context

This Agile Team Charter defines how a software development team can work together to deliver useful, reliable, and maintainable software. It establishes a shared purpose, core values, working agreements, responsibilities, and quality expectations to support collaboration and reduce misunderstandings.

This charter is an independently created academic artefact for my digital portfolio. It applies Agile concepts covered in my studies and draws on lessons from my industry placement at Achievements Awards Group (AAG). My workplace experience has helped me understand how communication, planning, feedback, testing, and collaboration contribute to software development.

The charter is intended to guide team behaviour while allowing the team to review and improve its agreements as its needs change.

## 2. Shared purpose and intended value

### Team purpose

Our purpose is to deliver software improvements that address user and business needs while maintaining the reliability, security, and maintainability of the systems we work on.

We aim to understand the problem before implementing a solution, collaborate throughout development, and verify that completed work meets agreed requirements.

### Who benefits and how?

- **Users:** Receive software that addresses their needs and behaves as expected.
- **Business stakeholders:** Gain improvements that support business processes and objectives.
- **Development team:** Benefits from clear expectations, shared knowledge, constructive feedback, and fewer avoidable misunderstandings.
- **The wider organisation:** Benefits from maintainable solutions, responsible handling of information, and reduced risk of introducing defects.

### What success looks like

Success is not measured only by the number of tasks completed. Work should deliver the intended value, meet agreed acceptance criteria, and satisfy the team's quality expectations. Progress should be visible, issues should be raised early, and the team should use feedback to improve both its product and its way of working.

## 3. Core values and working principles

The following values guide how team members are expected to work together.

| Value | Expected behaviour |
|---|---|
| Collaboration | Share relevant knowledge, ask questions, and involve the appropriate people when making decisions. |
| Transparency | Communicate progress, uncertainty, risks, and blockers honestly and promptly. |
| Respect | Listen to different viewpoints, give constructive feedback, and treat team members professionally. |
| Accountability | Take responsibility for assigned work, communicate when commitments are at risk, and follow through on agreed actions. |
| Quality | Consider correctness, maintainability, security, and testing rather than focusing only on completing a task quickly. |
| Adaptability | Respond constructively to new information and changing requirements, reassessing priorities when necessary. |
| Continuous improvement | Reflect on outcomes, learn from mistakes, and adjust working practices when there is a clear reason to do so. |

These values should influence everyday decisions, not remain statements that exist only in a document.

## 4. Roles and responsibilities

Team members may have different areas of responsibility, but successful delivery depends on collaboration rather than working in isolation.

| Role | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Product owner or business representative | Clarifies the problem, communicates stakeholder needs, helps prioritise work, and provides feedback on whether the outcome meets the intended need. |
| Developers | Analyse requirements, design and implement solutions, test changes, communicate technical concerns, and maintain the quality of the software. |
| Team facilitator or Agile lead, where applicable | Helps the team coordinate its work, encourages participation, and helps identify or address impediments. |
| Reviewer or technical mentor | Provides constructive feedback, supports technical decision-making, and helps identify maintainability, correctness, or design concerns. |
| Tester or verification role, where applicable | Checks the solution against expected behaviour, explores relevant edge cases, and communicates defects or gaps. |

These roles are illustrative rather than rigid job boundaries. Depending on the team and project, one person may perform more than one role. Team members should help one another where appropriate while keeping responsibilities and decisions clear.

## 5. Communication and working agreements

The team will use the following agreements to coordinate work and avoid preventable misunderstandings.

### 5.1 Daily communication

- Team members provide concise updates during agreed check-ins, covering progress, planned work, and blockers.
- Important blockers or risks are raised promptly rather than being left until the next scheduled meeting.
- Updates focus on coordination and problem-solving rather than becoming lengthy technical discussions.
- When an issue requires a deeper discussion, the relevant team members arrange a follow-up conversation.

### 5.2 Clarification and decision-making

- Before implementation begins, the person responsible for the work should clarify unclear requirements and acceptance criteria.
- Technical assumptions should be checked against available requirements, documentation, system behaviour, or appropriate testing.
- Significant decisions and changes to agreed requirements should be communicated to the people affected.
- When team members disagree, they should discuss the evidence, explain their reasoning, and work towards a decision that supports the project goal.

### 5.3 Collaboration and feedback

- Team members ask for assistance when they are blocked or when an issue extends beyond their current understanding.
- Feedback should address the work and its reasoning, not attack the individual.
- Review comments should be considered carefully, and unresolved concerns should be discussed respectfully.
- Knowledge gained while solving a problem should be shared when it could help the team avoid similar issues.

### 5.4 Respect for time and commitments

- Meetings should begin and end as close to the agreed times as practical.
- Team members should respect the time allocated to planning, development, and review.
- If a commitment is at risk, the responsible person should communicate the situation early and help the team agree on the next step.
- The team should aim for a sustainable pace rather than relying on repeated last-minute work.

## 6. Planning and flow of work

The team should maintain a shared understanding of what needs to be done, why it matters, and what is currently in progress.

### 6.1 Work preparation and prioritisation

- Work should be described clearly enough for the team to understand the problem and expected outcome.
- Priorities should reflect stakeholder value, urgency, dependencies, and available capacity.
- Large or unclear items should be discussed and broken into smaller, manageable pieces where appropriate.
- Estimates are used to support planning and shared understanding, not as guarantees of exact completion time or as a measure of individual performance.

### 6.2 Sprint planning

At the beginning of a sprint, the team should:

1. Review the highest-priority work and clarify the expected outcomes.
2. Discuss capacity, dependencies, risks, and any unresolved questions.
3. Agree on a realistic sprint goal.
4. Select work that the team believes it can reasonably progress during the sprint.
5. Identify the tasks, checks, and coordination needed to deliver the selected work.

The plan should guide the team's work without preventing reasonable adjustments when new information emerges.

### 6.3 During the sprint

- Work progress should remain visible to the team.
- Team members should coordinate when one task depends on another.
- New requests should be assessed for their urgency and impact rather than automatically disrupting existing commitments.
- The team should avoid starting excessive work simultaneously when doing so would delay completion or make blockers harder to identify.

### 6.4 Review and retrospective

At the end of a sprint or another agreed delivery period, the team should review the outcome and reflect on its process.

- **Review:** Examine the completed work, compare it with the expected outcome, and gather relevant feedback.
- **Retrospective:** Discuss what worked well, what created difficulties, and what could be improved.
- **Follow-through:** Agree on practical improvement actions and revisit them to determine whether they helped.

The purpose of these activities is to improve delivery and teamwork, not to assign blame.

## 7. Quality expectations: Definition of Ready and Definition of Done

Clear readiness and completion criteria help the team develop a consistent understanding of when work can begin and when it can be considered complete. The following criteria are proposed for this academic example and should be adapted to the needs of the actual project.

### 7.1 Definition of Ready

A work item is ready to be considered for development when:

- The problem or user need is described clearly.
- The expected behaviour and acceptance criteria are sufficiently understood.
- The scope is manageable or has been broken into smaller items where appropriate.
- Important dependencies, constraints, and uncertainties have been identified.
- The team has a reasonable understanding of how the outcome will be verified.
- Necessary clarification, access, or information has been identified and is available or has an agreed plan.

If an item is not ready, the team should clarify it rather than proceed on assumptions that could lead to avoidable rework.

### 7.2 Definition of Done

Work is considered done when:

- The implementation meets the agreed requirements and acceptance criteria.
- Appropriate tests and verification checks have been completed for the change.
- Relevant error conditions, edge cases, and empty or loading states have been considered where applicable.
- The change has undergone the agreed review process, and required review feedback has been addressed.
- The change follows the project's applicable coding, security, and maintainability expectations.
- Relevant documentation or supporting information has been updated where necessary.
- The work has been integrated or submitted through the agreed workflow.
- Required deployment or staging verification has been completed when applicable.
- The work status and any remaining limitations are communicated accurately.

A task should not be described as fully complete when important checks remain outstanding. Where an item cannot satisfy a criterion, the team should communicate the limitation and agree on the appropriate next step rather than conceal the gap.

## 8. Handling blockers, disagreements, and changing requirements

Problems are a normal part of software development. The team's response should focus on understanding the issue and finding an appropriate solution.

### Blockers and technical problems

When a blocker occurs, the responsible team member should:

1. Describe the problem and its impact.
2. Identify what has already been checked and what remains uncertain.
3. Gather relevant evidence, such as reproducible steps, expected versus actual behaviour, or appropriate technical information.
4. Ask the relevant team member for assistance when needed.
5. Communicate the agreed action and update the work status.
6. Verify the solution and share the outcome.

### Disagreements

- Team members should explain their reasoning and listen to alternative viewpoints.
- Decisions should consider requirements, evidence, technical consequences, and project priorities.
- If the team cannot resolve a disagreement, it should involve the appropriate decision-maker or technical mentor.
- Once a decision is made, the reasoning should be communicated to those affected.

### Changing requirements

When new information changes the expected outcome, the team should clarify the change, assess its impact on existing work, and agree on any necessary reprioritisation. Adaptability should not mean making uncommunicated changes that leave stakeholders or other developers with conflicting expectations.

## 9. Boundaries and responsibilities

The team should work within the project's agreed constraints and respect its responsibilities to users, stakeholders, and the organisation.

- **Confidentiality:** Sensitive company, client, customer, and personal information must be handled responsibly and must not be exposed in public portfolio evidence.
- **Security:** Access permissions, credentials, and security requirements must be respected. Shortcuts that expose information or weaken system security are unacceptable.
- **Scope:** Work should remain aligned with the agreed objectives. Significant scope changes should be discussed with the appropriate stakeholders.
- **Technical responsibility:** Changes should account for relevant dependencies, existing behaviour, and potential effects on other parts of the system.
- **Honest reporting:** Progress, testing results, limitations, and completion status should be represented accurately.
- **Escalation:** Issues outside a team member's authority or expertise should be raised with the appropriate person rather than handled through unsafe assumptions.

These boundaries help the team balance delivery speed with responsible engineering.

## 10. Reflection: What I learned from industry experience

My industry placement has helped me understand that effective Agile development involves more than dividing work into tasks and attending meetings. Requirements, communication, technical design, testing, and feedback all influence the quality of the final result.

One useful learning experience involved a password-reset feature that required the front end to interact with backend functionality. My initial focus was on making the user interface perform the required actions. Feedback from a more experienced developer highlighted the importance of organising API calls within the appropriate application models and stores rather than placing that responsibility directly in the interface.

This feedback helped me recognise that a feature can appear to work while still having design issues that affect maintainability and separation of concerns. I began to consider not only whether an implementation met the immediate requirement, but also where its responsibilities belonged and how the design would affect future changes.

The experience also reinforced the value of asking questions, reviewing technical assumptions, and treating feedback as an opportunity to improve. These lessons informed the working agreements in this charter, particularly those concerning clarification, collaboration, technical quality, and constructive review.

In future work, I intend to clarify requirements and acceptance criteria before implementation, consider the responsibilities of each application layer, and identify suitable verification steps earlier. I will also communicate uncertainty and blockers more promptly rather than waiting until they affect progress.
